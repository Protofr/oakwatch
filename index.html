Symptom Check

5 questions about your tree. Takes about a minute.

Step 1: Checklist

Question 1 of 5 What kind of oak is it?

Live oaks have small, oval, leathery leaves that stay green almost all year. Red oaks (Texas red oak, Shumard oak) have bigger leaves with pointy lobes.

Question 2 of 5 Are the veins on the leaves turning yellow or brown?

Pick a few leaves and look at the thin lines (the veins). With oak wilt they go yellow, then brown, while the rest of the leaf stays green. On live oaks this is the biggest giveaway.

Question 3 of 5 Are leaves browning from the tips or edges?

The leaf goes pale or brown from the tip or edge inward. Red oaks with oak wilt often do this, but so do trees in a drought.

Question 4 of 5 Is the tree suddenly dropping lots of leaves?

A lot of leaves falling over a few weeks, in spring or summer when oaks normally hold on to them. The fallen leaves are often still partly green. (Live oaks also drop old leaves every spring as new ones grow in. That part is normal.)

Question 5 of 5 Have nearby oaks died recently?

Oak wilt usually kills oaks in a cluster that keeps getting bigger, because it travels through the roots. A few dead or dying oaks close together is a red flag.

Find an Oak Wilt Specialist

Keep watching your oaks

  • Look at a few leaves every couple of weeks in spring and early summer. That's when oak wilt is easiest to see.
  • Look for veins turning yellow or brown on live oak leaves.
  • Watch for groups of oaks dying in your neighborhood.
  • Paint every pruning cut and wound right away. Any paint works.
  • If something changes, run this check again.

This isn't a diagnosis. Only a lab test or an arborist can confirm oak wilt.

Safe to Prune?

Why does this matter?

Oak wilt is a fungus that kills oaks, mostly live oaks and red oaks. It spreads two ways:

  • Beetles: Small sap beetles pick up the fungus from sick trees and carry it to fresh cuts on healthy ones. They're most active in spring.
  • Roots: Oaks near each other often have roots that grow together. Once one tree has it, the fungus can move through the roots to the next tree, even into a neighbor's yard.

So one badly timed cut can end up hurting trees all down the block.

About

Why I built it

My dad is a huge gardener, and he also got me into gardening when I was young (around 9 or 10). Ever since then, gardening has been something we love doing together. Whether it was planting something new, taking care of our backyard, or just checking out how everything was growing, it became one of the best ways we spent time together and bonded.

We have many, many oak trees in our backyard and even more behind it. There’s even more just around our neighborhood. Growing up, I didn’t think much of them since they’d always been there. Over time, my dad and I noticed something very sad. Oak trees around our neighborhood were dying, and the population had decreased significantly. We first believed it was because of the droughts, or the Texas heat. Later, we learned about the disease called oak wilt, a deadly fungal disease that affects oak trees. We even ordered a lab test on some of our own trees, which confirmed this disease on our own trees.

What made me the most angry was that we couldn’t do anything about it. We watched our neighbors lose trees they had owned since before I was even born! We lost our own as well. There were treatments, like fungicide injections, but they were expensive and could not be used to save trees that were already sick. Lab tests to even identify these in the first place were even more expensive. The early symptoms could be mistaken for drought stress.

I also learned that oak wilt wasn’t just a problem affecting homeowners, in some oak species, it spread underground through connected root systems, meaning one infected tree in my backyard could spread to my neighbors. This made me realize something very important. Even if my dad and I did everything to protect our trees, we couldn’t solve the problem all by ourselves, we needed our neighbors to be involved.

This is what inspired me to create OakWatch. I wanted to use my computer science skills to solve a problem that affected me firsthand. The idea was simple, an app where homeowners could easily identify this issue, keep track of affected trees, and communicate with neighbors before the disease spreads further.

Instead of having everyone figure things out by themselves, I wanted a community where people could share observations, identify which trees were at risk, and make the right decisions. OakWatch isn’t meant to replace a lab test or arborist diagnosis.

What makes this so meaningful to me is that it happened in my own backyard. I was trying to help my dad. The first time I saw the look on his face after our trees suffered made something change inside me. It wasn’t a hypothetical problem. It was something that would make my dad proud and help him out.

Hopefully OakWatch can expand outside of just our community one day and help other families facing similar issues!

Privacy

  • Report locations are rounded to about 100 meters, so no pin points to an exact house.
  • "Check Near Me" uses your location on your phone to measure distances, then forgets it. It's never saved or sent anywhere.
  • OakWatch never asks for names, emails, or addresses.
  • Symptom Check photos stay on your phone. They're never uploaded.

The photo check

The photo check is a small image model made with Google's Teachable Machine, and it runs on your phone. It was trained on 116 photos identified by forest experts: oak wilt, healthy oak leaves, and other leaf problems that look a lot like oak wilt. It does make mistakes, so it only counts as one checklist answer.
